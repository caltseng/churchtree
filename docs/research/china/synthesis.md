# Historical synthesis

This original synthesis uses the [scope and evidence-status rules](README.md).
Citation links identify the passages actually inspected, including edited
translations and interested institutional testimony.

## Tang evidence: presence, translation and the limits of a monument

The secure starting point is not an apostolic legend but a surviving historical
object. A Chinese/Syriac monument erected in 781 narrates the arrival of
Olopun/Alopen in Chang'an in 635 and an imperial authorization in 638. Its
Christian language, clerical names and imperial eulogy situate a Church of
the East community in a multilingual imperial world. Chinese museum material
names its author 景净 (Jingjing). "Nestorian" is a conventional historical
label in older translations, not a sufficient or neutral definition of the
community's theology. The Church of the East must not be conflated with
Eastern Orthodoxy. [C01-C04](sources.md#c01)

The inscription is strong evidence for the community presenting itself in
781; it is later testimony about 635 and 638. It cannot alone tell us the
number of converts, the proportion of Iranian/Central Asian migrants and
local adherents, or how closely its imperial language matched conditions
outside patronage networks. The modern debate about diaspora versus
indigenization is important, but the inaccessible Deeg study has not been
treated here as fully reviewed scholarship. The 845 suppression context and
the fate of particular communities require further verification. Neither
"everything vanished in 845" nor an uninterrupted Tang-to-Yuan succession
is warranted by this packet. [C01-C04](sources.md#c01);
[medieval gaps](coverage.md#uncertainty-and-missing-neighborhood-register)

## Yuan encounters are not a continuation line

The Syriac history of Rabban Sawma supplies a different starting point from
European arrival biographies. Its thirteenth-century setting includes Christian
parents in Khanbaliq, ecclesiastical teachers, metropolitan bishops, monasteries
and Markos's archdeacon father in Kawshang. Sawma's parents resist their only
son's renunciation; Markos seeks him out for training; communities and patrons
help the travelers leave for western holy places. Christianity here is
household life, education, ascetic aspiration and outward connection, not only
the arrival of foreigners. Yet this is retrospective hagiographic narration
through Budge's translation, not a population survey, and the edition itself
disagrees about Kawshang's modern location. It establishes neither an exact
departure year nor a Tang-to-Yuan institutional bridge.
[C40](sources.md#c40); H55, E43-E45, R26/R42

The Quanzhou inscriptions still need direct scholarly edition review. An indexed
study of Mar Solomon's epitaph remains a lead, not permission to claim a merger
of Manichaeans and Christians. A distinct primary tradition is John of
Montecorvino's Franciscan report dated
1305-01-08. It describes a church at Cambalec, translation into a "Tartar"
language and an asserted body of converts. Its figures are missionary
self-report, and its report of purchasing children for training raises
questions of coercion which a success-only mission story would conceal.
[C05](sources.md#c05); [unread medieval leads](sources.md#discovery-leads-not-verified-evidence-for-release)

Marignolli's later recollections describe Franciscan churches, an archbishop's
residence, imperial support and worship at Cambalec. Read beside the Syriac
narrative, this counters a single-tradition medieval story without treating
travelers from different periods as acquaintances or all eastern Christians
as Latin converts. Both the Latin triumphal account and the Syriac exemplary
monastic life select what matters to their authors. Imperial support, changing
routes and warfare belong in the explanation, but these passages do not settle
the relative causes or timing of particular communities' disappearance.
[C41](sources.md#c41), [C40](sources.md#c40)

Montecorvino's letter establishes a Catholic mission in a Yuan setting. It does not
establish that all earlier Eastern Christians became Catholic, that the
mission's numerical claims were independently checked, or that its institutions
survived into the later Jesuit enterprise. The public selection must preserve
distinct documented episodes and conspicuous evidence gaps, not one national
"Christian Church of China" trunk.

## Ming and Qing Catholic histories: accommodation, conflict and Chinese agency

Macao's diocese, established in 1576 according to its historical collection,
belonged to a transregional Catholic jurisdiction. A jurisdictional grant was
not the same as effective control over every place named. Ricci's Macau arrival
in 1582 and Beijing residence in 1601 identify a renewed mission setting,
not the beginning of Christianity in China. Xu Guangqi's collaboration with
Ricci on the first six books of Euclid in 1607 and his later calendar work
show Chinese intellectual agency; the history should not reduce him to a
passive recipient of European science or equate mathematical collaboration
with ecclesiastical succession. [C09](sources.md#c09), [C10](sources.md#c10)

The Chinese Rites controversy exposes disagreements over whether particular
ancestral and Confucian practices were civil/social or incompatible religious
acts, together with questions of naming and authority. The 1692 Kangxi edict,
1715 Clement XI restrictions and 1721 imperial response are distinct documents.
The 1715 text also permits some customs judged non-superstitious; this was not
simply an argument about Latin versus Chinese liturgy or a ban on everything
Chinese. Yongzheng's separate 1724 prohibition must not be backdated to 1721.
[C06](sources.md#c06), [C07](sources.md#c07), [C08](sources.md#c08),
[C25](sources.md#c25)

An edict does not prove that lay religious life everywhere stopped. Conversely,
the persistence of some Catholic villages would not establish a continuous
nationwide bureaucracy. Chambon's historical synthesis makes family-resident
consecrated women visible: Petronilla Chen's vows are dated to 1643; beatas
teach women, baptize, tend sick people and maintain places of worship.
Religious service need not entail departure from family and village into a
European-style convent. Celibacy can challenge marriage expectations, while
missionaries' later efforts to standardize congregations can conflict with
local religious forms. These are forms of Chinese female agency and internal
church power, not a claim that every woman enjoyed autonomy or every region
shared one institution. The historical section depends on earlier scholarship;
its modern ethnography does not directly document a seventeenth-century vow.
[C42](sources.md#c42); H57, E47-E48, R27

This partly repairs rural/women's coverage but does not replace village-specific
evidence for eighteenth-century persistence. Harrison's village study and
King's work on Candida Xu remain next reads, not falsely passage-verified works.
The proposed 1939 rites-instruction and 1946 hierarchy milestones remain
unverified documentary leads, requiring direct passage/date and reception
review before a fuller Catholic institutional timeline.
[coverage priorities](coverage.md#uncertainty-and-missing-neighborhood-register)

## Orthodoxy: another eastern history, with its own institutional ruptures

The Russian/Albazinian story should occupy a distinct lane from Jingjiao.
An Orthodox compilation describes the 1685 transfer of Albazin captives to
Beijing and the first mission delegation's arrival in 1715. An ecclesial
report instead dates mission establishment to 1713; establishment and arrival
may describe different acts, but the underlying records remain to be checked.
Russian migration and competing jurisdictions after 1917 complicate any
single succession line. [C28](sources.md#c28), [C29](sources.md#c29),
[C30](sources.md#c30)

Likewise, a 1956 synodal autonomy decision and 1957 implementation/consecration
should not be silently collapsed into one founding date. The last Chinese
bishops' deaths in the 1960s and Cultural Revolution attacks interrupted
ordinary institutional life. The same confessional sources distinguish closed
churches from surviving belief. Their testimony is important, but shared possible
antecedents and lack of direct synodal-act review limit confidence in precise
jurisdictional claims.
The legible English translation of Pozdnyaev's chapter separates the November
1956 project, Vasily's May 1957 consecration and the intended council election
which, in that account, did not occur amid conflict with Simeon. It also
describes property transfers, emigration and damage involving Soviet as well
as Chinese authorities: decline cannot be reduced to the Cultural Revolution
alone. Its assignment of blame and jurisdictional legitimacy remains a
Moscow-associated interpretation; failed retrieval of ROCOR counteraccounts
does not validate it. E69-E71 separate a mission and named Chinese leaders
from E12's broad container, but no complete émigré succession tree is approved.
No current population estimate or parish map is imported.
[C29-C31](sources.md#c29), [C58](sources.md#c58)

## Protestant missions, Chinese translators and unequal treaties

Morrison arrived at Guangzhou in September 1807. Protestant translation and
printing depended on Chinese collaborators as well as foreign support.
Liang Fa's trajectory as printer, convert, evangelist, ordained minister and
author makes indigenous agency visible well before twentieth-century
"indigenization." The Chinese-language biography dates his baptism to
1816-11-03, ordination to 1823 and *勸世良言* to 1832. Those dates derive from
secondary presentation of mission literature, not a newly inspected ordination
register. Its internally inconsistent age at baptism is withheld.
[C11](sources.md#c11), [C12](sources.md#c12), [C13](sources.md#c13)

The influence of Liang's tract on Hong Xiuquan and the Taiping movement needs
a separate, contested reception history. It is not a denominational split
from the London Missionary Society and not an explanation of the rebellion
by one pamphlet alone. Nor did foreign access arise in a political vacuum.
Article XXIX of the U.S.-China treaty signed **June 18, 1858** extends protection
language to Christian teaching and converts. The official text contradicts
a modern scholarly article's July signing date. That article remains useful
for the tensions among mission, diplomacy and translation, but is not
infallible evidence for its incidental dates. Signing, ratification,
proclamation and local enforcement are different events.
[C14](sources.md#c14), [C15](sources.md#c15)

Treaty protection formed part of an unequal political setting, but that does
not make all Chinese converts foreign agents or all diplomats and missionaries
one actor. Yale's Boxer exhibit makes both Chinese Christian and foreign
missionary deaths visible in 1900. Its missionary-centered collections do not
by themselves resolve the Qing court's changing relationship with Boxers,
local violence, imperial military intervention or national victim totals.
These require further Chinese and comparative scholarship.
[C15](sources.md#c15), [C16](sources.md#c16)

### Inland mission, relief and local negotiation were not one method

Hudson Taylor's China Inland Mission, founded in 1865, combined evangelical
nondenominational belief, faith-finance, China-based administration and
recruitment of single women for interior work. This was an organization with
an evangelistic strategy, not the creation of one Chinese denomination.
The biography's Wang Laijun ordination in 1867 and its Chinese coworkers
complicate a Taylor-only story, but the sources do not establish equal control
over policy. Chinese dress and language learning could coexist with foreign
authority and restrictive expectations of women's education and family roles.
The mission biographies' admiration is evidence of their interpretation,
not independent proof of emancipation or absence of imperial entanglement.
[C44](sources.md#c44), [C45](sources.md#c45)

Timothy Richard's famine relief in 1876-1879 led him toward elite engagement,
scientific education and reform publishing; his 1891 literature-society
appointment is not that society's founding. Stanley describes criticism from
BMS colleagues of both strategy and theology. Richard's own 1916 introduction
joins spiritual salvation with science, commerce and national transformation,
while portraying Chinese knowledge and custom in hierarchical civilizational
terms. Humanitarian action and such paternalism can coexist. This differs
from Taylor's evangelistic/training emphasis without requiring a denominational
split or a verdict that one alone modernized China. No famine deaths,
relief-induced conversions or single-person reform causation are inferred.
The biographical header giving Richard's death as 1915 is rejected: Stanley
identifies that as retirement and dates his life to 1919.
[C47](sources.md#c47), [C48](sources.md#c48), [C59](sources.md#c59)

Lottie Moon's Shandong work also differs by place and audience. Her biography
distinguishes 1879 rural trips from 1885 Pingdu residence, describes women's
hymn/doctrine classes and local men inviting her to teach in 1887, and connects
the work to women's overseas support. These episodes illuminate restricted
female authority, rural reception and transnational resources, not just heroic
arrival. Local invitations matter, but one missionary's retrospective success
narrative cannot stand for every hearer's motives or establish retention.
Superlative "first" claims, fundraising totals and the death-by-self-starvation
interpretation are withheld pending independent comparison.
[C46](sources.md#c46)

## Republican plurality: union, revival, women and independent organization

Indigenous Christianity did not take one organizational form. Dora Yu built
independent preaching and training work and influenced the young Watchman
Nee. The sources identify a 1909 Bible study/prayer institution and a 1916
school, not one institution which must be the parent of all later assemblies.
Nee's early gatherings and a 1932 co-worker arrangement are different date
bases. Women co-workers such as Peace Wang and Ruth Lee must not disappear
behind a founder-only narrative; their independent biographies and the
assemblies' later divisions remain research gaps.
[C17](sources.md#c17), [C21](sources.md#c21)

The True Jesus Church formed in 1917 in a setting of Chinese biblical reading
and Pentecostal contacts. Pan Zhao's study shows that Wei's prior contacts do
not explain away the church's own interpretation of baptism, the Spirit,
Sabbath, footwashing and biblical authority. Claims of healing or divine
authorization are the participants' religious testimony. A 1924 seminary
reported in a 1947 church publication is evidence of retrospective
institutional narration, not a surviving 1924 prospectus read for this packet.
[C18](sources.md#c18)

Jing Dianying's Jesus Family emerged through a 1921 cooperative and a 1926
school; the Chinese biography dates its name to 1927. Common property/work,
lengthy prayer, tongues/healing testimony and gender-separated communal
housing describe a rural religious way of life, not just another organization.
The Chinese account also names Chen Bixi and reports criticism of leadership
privilege. These dependent English/Chinese biographies do not adjudicate
those allegations; neither success narrative nor suppression should immunize
church authority from historical inquiry. The reported 1952 suppression, despite initial
Three-Self participation, also prevents a simple official/unofficial survival
binary. [C20](sources.md#c20), [C51](sources.md#c51)

John Sung's itinerant revival connects local churches and a wider Chinese/
Southeast Asian world rather than founding a single successor denomination.
His 1930-1933 Bethel association, subsequent independent itineration,
1935-1940 journeys and 1938 Methodist ordination have different date bases.
The biography describes men and women trained as lay preachers, rural Bible
instruction, music, confession and restitution, while reporting continued
church connections. Dramatic denunciation and public confession also raise
questions about spiritual authority and the experiences of hearers, not only
the preacher's effectiveness. Healing claims and enduring-conversion totals
remain religious testimony. The source's placement of Dora Yu in Bethel
leadership in 1933 conflicts with her 1931 death and is not adopted.
[C50](sources.md#c50), [C17](sources.md#c17)

A different strategy pursued national Protestant cooperation and union.
Cheng Jingyi's Edinburgh intervention, National Christian Council leadership
and role in the Church of Christ in China show Chinese Christian initiatives
in ecumenism. The 1922 council and 1927 union are not the same institution,
and neither is the China Christian Council founded in the PRC period.
The union's full immediate partner list has not been verified; the source's
aggregate count cannot become a reviewed merger tree. [C19](sources.md#c19)

Shared texts offer another kind of connection. The Chinese Union Version,
published in Shanghai on April 22, 1919 according to the Bible Society's
history, emerged from interdenominational cooperation and the work of Chinese
translators and distributors as well as missionary scholars. The named Chinese
contributors and colporteurs challenge a one-translator story. A shared
translation, however, did not merge its readers into one denomination.
The institution's claim of spiritual/numerical impact is not an independent
population measure. [C38](sources.md#c38)

### Local languages and shared worship

Guo and Wang's hymnody study moves beyond a Mandarin Bible/famous-translator
account. E. C. Lord's 1856 Ningbo hymnbook combines Chinese characters,
Romanization and musical notation for ordinary services, baptism and communion.
The 1936 *Hymns of Universal Praise* represents six churches' cooperation,
not their merger. Chao Tzu-ch'en's 1931 morning poem, Hu De'ai's 1934 tune
and Bliss Wiant's 1936 harmony show distinct Chinese literary/musical and
cross-cultural contributions, with rural devotional language rather than
only imported hymn forms. Later overseas versions illustrate circulation,
not timeless identical texts. The article's inconsistent hymn arithmetic is
withheld; neither the original hymnbook nor musical rights were reviewed for
reproduction. [C43](sources.md#c43); H58/H71, E61-E63/E72, R34-R35

Big Flowery Miao Christians expose another limitation of a Han/coastal template.
The Pollard biography describes Miao envoys seeking instruction, Stephen Lee's
language work and local teachers, preachers and locally funded worship. It also
portrays missionary control of conduct and appeal to officials/treaty protection.
Its ethnic/sexual stereotypes and liberator rhetoric cannot be adopted as neutral
accounts of Nosu-Miao-Chinese social relations. UBS dates a New Testament to
1917 and describes a 1990 local OT team, support from 1992 and a 2009 full Bible,
using Chinese translations and original-language checking. Local-language
preaching and hymns matter because Chinese-language comprehension is uneven.
This is neither Pollard single-handedly inventing literacy nor all Miao/Hmong
languages sharing one history. Publication gaps do not prove an absence of
religious life, and institutional donor narratives are not demographic evidence.
[C55](sources.md#c55), [C56](sources.md#c56); H64-H67, E64-E67, R36-R37

Catholic leadership reform also matters. The 1924 Shanghai council promoted
Chinese clergy within a still largely foreign-led episcopate, followed by
six Chinese episcopal ordinations in October 1926. These were not the first
Chinese bishops in all history. A 2024 papal commemoration expresses a later
institutional interpretation of 1924, not a second contemporary witness to
the council. Wartime history requires more than a denominational roster:
Yale's Minnie Vautrin collections point to women's educational and humanitarian
work during the occupation, but the archive introduction alone does not
authorize detailed rescue totals. The separate Smith archival narrative adds
Ginling's five sponsoring mission boards, Wu Yi-fang's 1928 presidency,
1938 exile at West China Union University in Chengdu, and 1951 combination
with University of Nanking after financial ties were cut in 1950. Sponsorship,
Chinese women's governance, Vautrin's work in occupied Nanjing, displaced
teaching and state restructuring are not one institutional act. Nor does an
educational combination mean its supporting churches merged. These are read
archival historical summaries, not the underlying correspondence or diaries.
Student testimony, wartime medicine/relief elsewhere and fuller Chinese
institutional accounts remain needed.
[C22](sources.md#c22), [C23](sources.md#c23), [C37](sources.md#c37),
[C57](sources.md#c57)

## PRC ruptures, reopening and contested categories

The 1950s brought the departure/expulsion of foreign personnel and intense
reorganization under the new state. National Protestant and Catholic patriotic
associations were established in 1954 and 1957 respectively. The older idea
of self-support, self-government and self-propagation is not identical to the
entire later state-associated movement. Pius XII's 1954 response both supports
Chinese clergy/local resources and rejects separation from papal governance;
it is a participant's position, not a neutral account of the conflict.
[C24](sources.md#c24), [C32](sources.md#c32)

Wang Mingdao makes dissent historically specific. His household ministry and
1937 Tabernacle building predate the PRC; strict baptismal ethics, independent
finance and wartime refusal of an occupation-sponsored federation complicate
the idea that ecclesial self-government began with TSPM. The evangelical
biography attributes his later refusal to join TSPM partly to doctrinal
opposition to modernism, and reports a 1955 arrest, coerced confession,
1958 retraction/reimprisonment and end-1979 release. Theology, coercion and
state power intersect here; describing his own rationale is not proving
theology was the state's only concern. His influence does not establish
corporate descent of every unregistered church. The full 1955 text and prison
records were not inspected, and the biography's uncorroborated doctrinal
accusations and inconsistent duration wording are not imported.
[C49](sources.md#c49)

The Cultural Revolution's 1966-1976 suppression of public religious institutions
is a major rupture, not proof that all religious practice and belief ceased.
Reopening and revival in the reform period, followed by Document 19 in 1982,
combined limited recognized religious activity with continued state
boundaries. Formation of a national council is neither the start of Chinese
Protestantism nor proof that every independent body merged into one church.
Contemporary registered/unregistered networks do not form two neatly bounded,
internally uniform denominations. The WCC member profile dates the China
Christian Council to 1980 and describes cooperation with the TSPM; its
"post-denominational" description is explicitly within the CCC, not a census
of all Chinese Protestant identities. [C24](sources.md#c24),
[C25](sources.md#c25), [C39](sources.md#c39)

Ding Guangxun/K. H. Ting supplies a contrasting Chinese Christian voice, not
merely the name of an official leader. His October 1980 address argues for
patriotic self-government and improving church governance, support and
propagation. The WCC obituary recalls seminary, TSPM and CCC leadership and
renewed ecumenical connections in the 1980s-1990s. Chen Yilu's institutional
account dates the theological reconstruction decision to November 1998 and
presents cosmic Christ, divine love and reconciliation alongside adaptation
to socialist society and clergy education. Rebuilding involved theology and
formation as well as reopening buildings. These sources advocate/commemorate
that program; they do not prove all participants freely agreed or that dissenters
were unpatriotic. Wang's and Ding's accounts should not be collapsed into either
an all-collaborator or an all-harmonious national narrative. Neither the later
speeches nor the obituary are direct evidence adjudicating every 1950s dispute.
[C52](sources.md#c52), [C53](sources.md#c53), [C54](sources.md#c54),
[C49](sources.md#c49)

Catholic civil status, episcopal ordination and communion are also different
dimensions. Benedict XVI's 2007 letter distinguishes unregistered bishops,
government-associated bishops subsequently reconciled with Rome, and those
not reconciled. A single split edge between two permanently separate
"Catholic churches" would erase this complexity. The 2018 provisional agreement
concerned episcopal appointments; its actual text was not published in the
communiqué inspected here. Francis's subsequent reconciliation and stated
hopes are institutional acts, not proof that controversy ended. The October
2024 renewal extends the agreement four years; its reception and effects
require assessment beyond the parties' announcements.
[C33](sources.md#c33), [C34](sources.md#c34), [C35](sources.md#c35),
[C36](sources.md#c36)

The state's recent "Sinicization" campaign must not be retrojected as the
meaning of every earlier Chinese-led translation, rural revival or ecclesial
reform. They differ in actors, objectives and power. Equally, current national
religious statistics cannot be read as a single trajectory of uninterrupted
growth. Pew's comparison of surveys, official figures and advocacy estimates
shows how self-identification, belief, registration, age universes and survey
coverage produce very different measures. About 20 million adult Christian
self-identifiers in one 2018 survey and about 81 million adults reporting belief
in Jesus and/or Tianzhu in another are **not rival counts of the same population**.
The 2010/2018 self-identification difference was not statistically significant.
Neither averaging estimates nor extrapolating a future "largest Christian
country" follows from those findings. [C24](sources.md#c24),
[C26](sources.md#c26), [C27](sources.md#c27)

Retention and region require equally careful measures. Pew reports that about
a quarter of **2012 CFPS adult Christian identifiers** no longer identified in
2016, while about a third of **2016 identifiers** had not identified in 2012.
Different conditional denominators cannot be subtracted as a national net
conversion rate, and response changes are not known dates or permanent departures.
Its 72% female composition concerns 2018 CGSS self-identifiers, not all women or
all Christian histories. The 2004 registered-venue census distinguishes coastal
Protestant concentrations, inland Henan and Catholic Hebei, but counts sites,
not members or contemporary communities. Pew's discussion of rural outmigration
and urban/returnee churches suggests redistribution and roster double-counting;
the qualitative reports do not quantify a national migration effect. Declining
Catholic share, stable measured affiliation, continuing conversions and local
revival can coexist without a single national success/decline explanation.
[C26](sources.md#c26), [C27](sources.md#c27); D07-D09

## What this foundation does not claim

This is a writer-reviewed foundation with a substantial primary-document and
institutional-source base plus selected scholarship, not independent
Sinological, theological or archival certification. Early/medieval manuscript
and epigraphic scholarship, village-specific Catholic persistence, many Protestant
union partners, languages beyond the bounded Miao/Ningbo examples, ordinary
women's own testimony, competing Orthodox jurisdictions and 2025-2026 developments
still need work. The [question-led audit](audit.md) distinguishes repaired
episodes from these residual questions and from the smaller public selection.
These are explicit
limits on conversion to public timeline data, not blanks to be filled with
invented dates, inferred parentage or confident prose.
